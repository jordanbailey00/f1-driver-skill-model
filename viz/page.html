Equal Machinery

Put two drivers in the same car and the car stops mattering. This rating is built only from those moments: every qualifying lap and race result between teammates, chained through shared teammates so drivers who never met can still be compared.

The ratio

How much faster than an average driver, in the same car

The rating is the lap time a driver would gain on an average F1 driver of 1990–2026 if both drove the identical car, in today's lap-time terms. +0.50% is about 0.45 s on a 90-second lap. The equal-car ratio is the same thing as a ratio of lap times: 0.9950 means the driver needs 99.50% of the average driver's time. Bars show the 90% range.

How it's built

From one lap-time ratio to a single scale

Same car, same weekend

For every race since 1977, each pair of teammates is compared on their best qualifying lap from the deepest session both set a time in (Q3 if both reached it). A 1:30.000 against a teammate's 1:30.270 is a 0.30% gap. Refuelling-era Q3 laps (2006–09) and race-fuel sessions (2003–05) are swapped for the low-fuel session where one exists.

Chain the gaps

A beat B by 0.2% and B beat C by 0.3%, so A should be about 0.5% quicker than C. Solving all 10,000+ duels at once puts 316 drivers on one scale. Drivers with little data are pulled toward average until the evidence says otherwise.

Correct for learning, ageing and era

Rookies improve over roughly their first 50 starts, and drivers lose pace after about 30. Both curves are learned from the data, so a 40-year-old beating a rookie is credited properly. Gaps were also about 2.4× larger in the mid-1980s than today, for the same skill, so each era's gaps are rescaled to today's spread.

Add race day

Who finished ahead, counting only results the drivers controlled. Engine failures, gearbox failures and collisions are left out; spins, crashes and driver penalties count against the driver. This adds a small racecraft adjustment on top of pace.

One number per driver

The rating is a driver's career-average level with age and inexperience taken out, relative to the average starter of 1990–2026.

gap(A,B) = 100 × ln( lapB / lapA ) same car, same session gap(A,B) ≈ skill(A) − skill(B) + noise skill = prime + learning(starts) + ageing(age) + form(season) rating = average skill over a career, age and rookie effects removed ratio = exp(−rating/100) ≈ 1 − rating/100 lap time ÷ average driver's

Qualifying gaps use a heavy-tailed error, so a lap ruined by a red flag or a mechanical problem can't swing a rating. Gaps beyond 4% are dropped as car trouble. Race results enter through a probit model of who finished ahead.

What the rating can't see

Team orders and number-one status

A contracted number two who gave up track position, or got the older parts, looks slower than they really were. The rating has no way to know.

Small samples

A driver with a handful of races is pulled hard toward average and carries a wide range. Use the duels filter; the default hides drivers with fewer than 20.

Era scale is an assumption

It assumes gaps shrank because cars and preparation got more consistent, not because drivers got more alike. Treating a 1990 percent as a 2020 percent predicts worse, but would lift Senna, Prost and the 1980s drivers further.

The ageing curve is an average

Some drivers fade early and some, like Alonso, barely fade. A late career that holds up better than the curve lifts a rating; one that fades faster lowers it. The curve also sets how much credit a young driver gets for beating an older champion, so Russell's and Leclerc's ratings depend on how much Hamilton and Vettel had faded.

Islands in the network

Jules Bianchi and Max Chilton were only ever teammates with each other, so they can be compared with one another but not with anyone else. They are left out of the ranking.

Young drivers are projections

A rookie's rating includes the usual improvement still to come. Open circles in the ranking mark drivers active in 2026 with fewer than 60 starts.

Model: Student-t teammate-gap model with learning, ageing and era-scale terms, AR(1) season form and a probit race component, fitted by penalised maximum likelihood. Ranges from the Laplace approximation.
